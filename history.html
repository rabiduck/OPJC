Origins

A short history of judo

Judo was founded in Japan by Jigoro Kano in 1882, drawing on techniques and ideas from traditional schools of jujutsu while placing greater emphasis on safe practice, education and personal development.

Kano established the Kodokan in Tokyo as the centre of his new system. By restricting some of the more dangerous techniques found in older martial arts, judo allowed students to practise more freely against resisting partners. This helped make randori — free practice — a central part of training alongside formal practice known as kata.

Kano saw judo as much more than a method of fighting. He regarded it as a form of physical and moral education, built around the efficient use of physical and mental energy and the idea of mutual welfare and benefit.

Judo spread internationally during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, developing both as a traditional martial art and an organised competitive sport. It made its Olympic debut at the Tokyo Games in 1964 and remains one of the world’s major international martial arts sports.

Judo in Britain

From Japan to Britain.

Judo began to establish a lasting presence in Britain during the early twentieth century, with London becoming an important centre for its development in Europe.

In 1918, Gunji Koizumi founded the Budokwai in London. It became a major centre for Japanese martial arts and played an important part in the development of British and European judo.

Koizumi later helped establish the British Judo Association in 1948 and was also closely involved in the development of organised judo across Europe. From those early foundations, judo grew throughout Britain through clubs, instructors and generations of students passing on their knowledge.

That history provides the wider setting for Old Priory’s own story, which began in York in 1947.
